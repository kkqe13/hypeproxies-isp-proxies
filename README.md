## Why static ISP proxies are often the better fit for persistent sessions

There are several proxy categories, and the cheapest option is not automatically the cheapest after failed logins, broken sessions, or unusable capacity.

### Static ISP proxies

Static ISP proxies, sometimes called static residential proxies, use IP addresses registered with internet service providers while being hosted on server infrastructure. The key characteristic is persistence: the address stays assigned rather than changing every request.

That is useful when a legitimate task requires continuity, such as checking an authorized dashboard, conducting repeatable regional QA, or maintaining a stable connection for a company-controlled asset. The trade-off is simple: static IPs are normally purchased in fixed quantities, so they are not ideal for a one-off task or for large-volume work that truly requires a constantly changing pool of locations.

### Rotating residential proxies

Rotating residential services cycle traffic through different consumer-network IPs. They can be appropriate for permitted public-web research where requests are independent and the provider’s terms, target-site rules, and applicable law allow the activity.

They are generally a poor match for a logged-in session that needs to remain consistent. A changing connection during a workflow can trigger security checks or simply break the session. “More residential” does not automatically mean “more suitable.”

### Datacenter proxies

Datacenter proxies are commonly inexpensive and fast. They can be fine for low-risk tasks on services that allow them, but their network origin may be less appropriate for location-sensitive social-media QA.

A low sticker price can become false economy if the IP range has poor reputation, if your permitted task needs a particular US location, or if the provider cannot explain whether the IP is dedicated or shared.

## A compliant purchase checklist

Before buying any Facebook proxy service, run through this short checklist with the person responsible for your Meta assets:

1. **Define the permitted use case.** Write down what the connection is for: regional QA, access while travelling, public research, or another approved business purpose.
2. **Confirm account ownership and access.** Use Meta’s official roles and business permissions. Do not share credentials or use a proxy to conceal unauthorized access.
3. **Match the geography to the task.** If you need US-based testing, choose a US location that reflects the test scenario. Do not claim to be located somewhere for which you have no legitimate basis.
4. **Keep an internal assignment record.** Record which employee, client, or documented testing environment is associated with each proxy. This makes troubleshooting much less chaotic.
5. **Test a small sample first.** Verify connectivity, location, latency, authentication, and compatibility with your authorized software before deploying the full allocation.
6. **Follow platform limits and policies.** Do not automate prohibited actions, evade review, create inauthentic accounts, or use the service to get around a restriction.
7. **Have a support and exit plan.** Know how to contact the provider, how cancellation works, and what you will do if the service does not fit the intended workflow.
